Screening

Power strips, irons and travel kettles are commonly confiscated.

Prohibited-item lists vary by line and by ship. Surge-protected strips and anything with a heating element are the usual seizures — read your own line’s list before packing.

Self-inflicted delays

Four things that slow embarkation

01
Arriving before your window
The terminal fills with people whose time has not come yet, and everybody waits longer for it.
02
Items that must be searched
A power strip in a carry-on means your bag is opened and gone through by hand.
03
Documents in the hold bag
The one case you cannot reach is the one the desk asks you to open.
04
Boarding at the popular hour
The middle of the window is the crowd. The edges of it are quiet.

Not malice, arithmetic

What the terminal is actually doing

Arrival windows
Staggering guests is the only throughput mechanism a terminal really has.
Security screening
Every flagged bag becomes a hand search, and hand searches are slow.
Terminal capacity
Some ports are handling two or three ships across the same morning.
Manifest checks
Names, documents and forms are reconciled one guest at a time.

Stricter than a port day

Embarkation has a harder deadline than any port call.

Royal Caribbean, for instance, asks guests to be onboard no less than one hour before scheduled departure. Confirm the rule your own line applies.

The payoff

Take your window, carry your documents, leave the power strip at home.

That is most of it. The rest is arriving in the quieter half of your window and going straight up to lunch.
